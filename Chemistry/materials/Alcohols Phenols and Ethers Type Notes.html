Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• Phenol (or a substituted phenol) is treated with Br2, HNO3, H2SO4, CHCl3/NaOH or CO2/NaOH and the product, a count or an order is asked.

• Salicylaldehyde, salicylic acid, picric acid, aspirin or phenolphthalein appears in the stem or the options.

• Cumene, chlorobenzene with NaOH at high pressure, or 'phenol and acetone' is mentioned.

2. Formulae and laws

Bromination

Br2/H2O → 2,4,6-tribromophenol; Br2/CS2, low T → p-bromophenol (mainly)

Nitration

dil. HNO3 → o- + p-nitrophenol; conc. HNO3 (after conc. H2SO4) → picric acid (2,4,6-trinitrophenol)

Reimer–Tiemann

PhOH+CHCl3+NaOH→ salicylaldehyde (ortho; carbene :CCl2)

Kolbe

PhONa+CO2 (400 K, 4–7 atm), then H+ → salicylic acid

Cumene process

cumene + O2 → cumene hydroperoxide → H3O+ → phenol + acetone

Dow process

PhCl+NaOH (623 K, 300 atm) → PhONa → H+ → phenol

3. Concepts and skills

Why does phenol react faster than benzene in electrophilic substitution?

The lone pair on O is delocalised into the ring, so the ortho and para carbons become electron rich and the ring is activated.

Why does Br2 water give the tribromo product but Br2/CS2 the mono product?

In water phenol is partly ionised to phenoxide, which is far more reactive; in a non-polar solvent the activation is weaker.

What is the electrophile in the Reimer–Tiemann reaction?

Dichlorocarbene, :CCl2, formed from CHCl3 and base; it attacks the phenoxide ion at the ortho position.

Why does picric acid need two steps (sulphonation, then nitration) in good yield?

Direct nitration oxidises phenol; the SO3H groups protect the ring and are replaced by NO2.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Giving the mono-bromo product with bromine water.

• Forgetting that the Dow process starts from chlorobenzene and needs 623 K and 300 atm.

• Counting the oxygen atoms of the SO3H groups wrongly when the sulphonic acid is the product.

• Writing the para product for Reimer–Tiemann and Kolbe (ortho is major).

Completed notes – Type 2

1. How do I spot this type?

• An alkene, carbonyl compound or ester is converted to an alcohol and the reagents (BH3, Hg(OAc)2, LiAlH4) decide the structure.

• An alcohol is treated with SOCl2, PBr3, HX, PCC, Jones reagent or NaOI.

• A three- to five-step sequence ends in 'identify P' or 'number of carbons/atoms in the product'.

2. Formulae and laws

Hydration of alkenes

acid-catalysed: Markovnikov with rearrangement; Hg(OAc)2, then NaBH4: Markovnikov, no rearrangement; BH3, then H2O2/OH−: anti-Markovnikov

Reduction

LiAlH4: acid, ester, aldehyde, ketone → alcohol; NaBH4: aldehyde, ketone only

Alcohol → halide

ROH+SOCl2→ RCl+SO2+HCl; PBr3 → RBr (no rearrangement); HX: Lucas order 3°>2°>1°

Oxidation

PCC: 1°→ RCHO, 2°→ ketone; KMnO4 or Jones: 1°→ RCOOH

Haloform

RCH(OH)CH3 or RCOCH3 + NaOI → RCOONa+CHI3

Diol and glycerol

alkene + cold dil. KMnO4 → glycol; glycerol + KHSO4, heat → acrolein

3. Concepts and skills

Why does hydroboration give the anti-Markovnikov alcohol?

Boron adds to the less hindered carbon and hydride to the other; oxidation then replaces B by OH with retention.

Why is SOCl2 the best reagent to make an alkyl chloride from an alcohol?

The by-products SO2 and HCl are gases, so the product is easily isolated and rearrangement is avoided.

Why can a 3° alcohol not be oxidised without breaking carbon–carbon bonds?

The carbinol carbon has no hydrogen to lose.

How do you decide between NaBH4 and LiAlH4?

LiAlH4 is strong enough to reduce acids and esters; NaBH4 stops at aldehydes and ketones.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the Markovnikov product for hydroboration.

• Forgetting that PCC stops at the aldehyde.

• Missing a rearrangement when HX (not PBr3) is used on a 2° alcohol.

• Dropping a step in a sequence and miscounting the carbons.

Completed notes – Type 3

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Which pair of reagents gives the ether' or 'unsymmetrical ether is prepared by' is asked.

• An ether is heated with HI or HBr (excess) and the products are asked.

• Anisole, an epoxide or a benzylic ether undergoes a reaction other than cleavage.

2. Formulae and laws

Williamson

RONa+R'X→ ROR'+NaX (SN2: the halide must be 1° or methyl)

Alkoxide + 3° halide

elimination: alkene + alcohol, not an ether

Cleavage

ROR'+HI: I goes to the smaller (less hindered) group in SN2; to the 3°, benzyl or allyl group in SN1

Aryl alkyl ether

ArOR+HI→ ArOH+RI (never ArI)

Symmetrical ethers

2ROH→H2SO4,413KROR (1° alcohols)

Anisole

o/p directing and activating: Friedel–Crafts, nitration and bromination at o/p

3. Concepts and skills

Why can t−BuONa+CH3Br make methyl tert-butyl ether but not CH3ONa+t−BuBr?

The first uses a 1° halide in SN2; the second gives elimination to isobutene.

Why does HI cleave an aryl alkyl ether on the alkyl side?

The Caryl−O bond has partial double-bond character and the sp2 carbon cannot be attacked, so the iodide attacks the alkyl carbon.

When does cleavage follow SN1?

When one group is 3°, benzyl or allyl: the stable cation forms and the halide goes to that group.

Why are ethers stored away from air?

They slowly form explosive peroxides.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Choosing a 3° alkyl halide for the Williamson synthesis.

• Putting the halogen on the aryl carbon after cleavage.

• Adding the iodide to the larger group in an SN2 cleavage.

• Forgetting that excess HI also converts the alcohol into an iodide.

Completed notes – Type 4

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Arrange in increasing/decreasing order of acidic strength (pKa)' of phenols or alcohols is asked.

• A test is named (Lucas, neutral FeCl3, Br2 water, iodoform) or the observations are listed for an unknown compound.

• Boiling points or solubility in water, NaOH or NaHCO3 are compared.

2. Formulae and laws

Acidity

p-nitrophenol > phenol > water > 1° ROH > 2° > 3°

Substituent effect

−NO2, −CN, −X increase acidity; −CH3, −OCH3 decrease it

Lucas test

ZnCl2/HCl: 3° turbid at once, 2° in about 5 min, 1° on heating

Neutral FeCl3

phenols give violet, blue or green colour

Solubility

phenols dissolve in NaOH but not in NaHCO3 (weaker than carbonic acid)

Steam volatility

o-nitrophenol (intramolecular H-bond) is steam volatile; p-nitrophenol is not

3. Concepts and skills

Why is phenol more acidic than ethanol?

The phenoxide ion is stabilised by resonance; the ethoxide ion is destabilised by the +I effect of the ethyl group.

Why is pKa of a 3° alcohol higher than that of a 1° alcohol?

Three alkyl groups push electrons to O and crowd solvation of the alkoxide ion, so it is less stable.

Why do alcohols boil higher than ethers of the same molar mass?

Alcohols form intermolecular hydrogen bonds; ethers cannot.

What does a compound that dissolves in NaOH but not in NaHCO3 tell you?

It is a phenol (weakly acidic), not a carboxylic acid.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Reversing the order of acidity of 1°, 2° and 3° alcohols.

• Thinking an alcohol dissolves in NaOH.

• Applying the Lucas test to a 1° alcohol and expecting turbidity at once.

• Putting o-nitrophenol above p-nitrophenol in boiling point.

Completed notes – Type 5

1. How do I spot this type?

• An alcohol is heated with conc. H2SO4 or H+ and the major alkene or the number of alkenes is asked.

• A cyclobutyl or cyclopentyl carbinol is treated with acid ('ring expansion').

• A molecule with both an alcohol and an alkene is treated with H3O+.

2. Formulae and laws

Dehydration

ROH + conc. H2SO4, heat → alkene (E1); ease 3°>2°>1°

Zaitsev

the more substituted alkene is the major product

Carbocation shifts

1,2-H or 1,2-CH3 shift to a more stable cation (3°>2°>1°)

Ring expansion

strain relief: 4→5→6 membered rings

Counting alkenes

count each distinct alkene, with cis/trans isomers, from every cation

Ethanol

conc. H2SO4, 443 K → ethene; 413 K → diethyl ether

3. Concepts and skills

Why does a carbocation rearrange?

A shift of H or CH3 (or a ring expansion) gives a more stable cation, so the first-formed cation is not the one that reacts.

Why does a cyclobutylcarbinyl cation expand to a cyclopentyl cation?

Cyclobutane has large angle strain; the expansion relieves it and gives a more stable cation.

When the alcohol and an alkene are both present, which one is protonated first?

The alkene, which gives a tertiary cation easily; the intramolecular OH can then close a ring. Ionising the alcohol is the competing, slower route.

Why is the major alkene the more substituted one?

It is more stable (hyperconjugation), and the transition state leading to it is lower in energy.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Forgetting to count cis/trans isomers.

• Stopping at the first cation when a shift gives a more stable one.

• Writing the Hofmann (less substituted) alkene for acid-catalysed dehydration.

• Expanding a ring when a hydride shift would give a better cation first.

Completed notes – Type 6

1. How do I spot this type?

• RMgX (or CH3MgBr, C2H5MgBr, PhMgBr) is added to an aldehyde, ketone, ester, epoxide or CO2.

• 'Which pair of reagents gives this alcohol' or 'which cannot be prepared by'.

• A sequence with Mg, then a carbonyl, then oxidation or dehydration ends in a number (mass %, moles, atoms).

2. Formulae and laws

HCHO

RMgX+HCHO→ RCH2OH (1°)

Aldehyde

RMgX+R'CHO→ RR'CHOH (2°)

Ketone or ester

RMgX (excess) → 3° alcohol (R2C(OH)R'; esters add two R groups)

CO2 / epoxide

→ RCOOH / alcohol with two more carbons

Acidic H

RMgX+ROH(orH2O,RCOOH,RC≡CH)→RH

Moles

n=m/M, then follow the 1:1 steps and convert back to mass or percentage

3. Concepts and skills

Why must a Grignard reagent be prepared in dry ether?

It is a strong base and reacts at once with water, giving the alkane.

Why does an ester give a 3° alcohol with excess RMgX?

The first addition expels R'O− to form a ketone, which reacts again with a second RMgX.

Why does a molecule with an –OH or –COOH group quench the reagent?

The acidic hydrogen is removed by the carbanion, forming RH and a magnesium salt.

With methyl vinyl ketone, how does RMgX usually add?

By 1,2-addition to the C=O, giving a tertiary allylic alcohol, since Cu(I) is needed for 1,4-addition.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Forgetting that esters take two moles of RMgX.

• Ignoring an acidic hydrogen in the substrate.

• Choosing HCHO when a 2° alcohol is the target.

• Using the wrong molar mass when a mass percentage is asked.
