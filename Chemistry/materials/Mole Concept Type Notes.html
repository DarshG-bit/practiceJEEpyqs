Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• A reaction is given (or described in words) and the mass, moles or volume of a product or reactant is asked.

• Two reactants are given in grams or moles, and the 'limiting reagent', 'excess' or the product formed is asked.

• 'Percentage yield', '92% yield', 'purity' or a sequence of reactions (two products P and Q) appears.

2. Formulae and laws

Moles from mass, from gas volume at STP, from number of particles

n=mM=V22.4L=NNA

Mole ratio for aA+bB→cC

nAa=nBb=nCc (for a complete reaction)

Mass of product C from mass of A

mC=mAMA×ca×MC

Gas volumes in a reaction at the same T and P

The volume ratio equals the mole ratio (Gay-Lussac and Avogadro)

Molar volume of a gas at STP (0∘C, 1 atm); at 1 bar

22.4L; 22.7L

Limiting reagent

The reactant with the smaller value of molescoefficient

Excess reagent left after the reaction

nleft=ninitial−nused (moles used from the mole ratio with the limiting reagent)

Combustion of a hydrocarbon

CxHy+(x+y4)O2→xCO2+y2H2O

Percentage yield

Mass of pure substance in an impure sample of purity p%

mpure=msample×p100

Overall yield of two steps with yields Y1 and Y2

Y=Y1×Y2 (as fractions)

3. Concepts and skills

What is the first step in every stoichiometry question?

Balance the equation, then convert the given quantity to moles; the coefficients give the mole ratio.

How do you find the limiting reagent quickly?

Divide the moles of each reactant by its coefficient: the smaller number belongs to the limiting reagent, which is used up completely.

A question gives 'excess' of one reagent. What does that tell you?

The other reagent is limiting, so all calculations use its moles.

How are two-step yields handled when a product of step 1 is the reactant of step 2?

Find the moles formed in step 1 using its yield, then use these moles (with the second yield) in step 2.

How do you treat a gas volume given at STP?

Convert with 22.4 L per mole; if the temperature or pressure is different, use PV=nRT.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the equation without balancing it.

• Comparing the moles of the two reactants directly instead of dividing by the coefficients.

• Taking the limiting reagent as the one with the smaller mass.

• Using 22.4 L per mole at a temperature other than STP.

• Applying the percentage yield to the wrong quantity (it applies to the product, not to the reactant).

3. Concepts and skills

What is the best way to convert between concentration terms?

Choose a basis: 1L of solution, 100g of solution or 1kg of solvent. Use the density to get the mass of 1L, then find the mass of solute and solvent.

Which concentration terms change with temperature?

Molarity, normality and volume percent (they depend on volume). Molality, mole fraction and mass percent do not.

How do you find the molarity of a mixture of an acid and a base?

Find the moles left after neutralisation (excess) and divide by the total volume.

What is the n-factor of an acid, a base and a salt in neutralisation?

Acid: number of H+ replaced; base: number of OH−; salt such as Na2CO3: charge on the cation or anion that reacts.

What is the meaning of '10 volume' hydrogen peroxide?

1mL of the solution releases 10mL of O2 at STP.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the volume of the solvent instead of the volume of the solution in M.

• Forgetting to convert grams of solvent to kilograms for molality.

• Using the density of the solvent (not of the solution) when converting between M and m.

• Averaging the mass percents without weighting by the masses.

• Forgetting the n-factor in neutralisation.

3. Concepts and skills

Why can the carbon and hydrogen in the sample be found from CO2 and H2O?

All the carbon of the compound ends up in CO2 and all the hydrogen in H2O on complete combustion.

How do you find the oxygen content in a combustion analysis?

By difference: it is the mass of the sample not accounted for by C and H.

What if the mole ratio is not a whole number?

Multiply by 2, 3 or 4 until all numbers are whole (for example 1.5→3 when multiplied by 2).

How does the molar mass help?

It gives the multiple n that turns the empirical formula into the molecular formula.

How do you handle a question with 'equivalents of oxygen'?

Treat equivalents as moles for the balanced equation: write the combustion equation and match the moles of O2 and H2O.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using 12/44 for hydrogen or 2/18 for carbon.

• Forgetting the mass of the sample in the denominator of the percentage.

• Rounding the mole ratio too early (for example 1.33→1).

• Giving the empirical formula when the molecular formula is asked.

3. Concepts and skills

How many atoms are in one mole of a diatomic or triatomic molecule?

2NA and 3NA: multiply the number of molecules by the atoms per molecule.

Which has more atoms in equal masses of the elements?

The element of smaller atomic mass (more moles in the same mass).

Same number of moles of different gases at the same T and P?

They occupy equal volumes (Avogadro's law).

How is the average atomic mass found from a given ratio of isotopes?

Weight the masses by the ratio; solve for the unknown ratio by setting the average equal to the given value.

How can a molar mass be found from weights of two compounds?

Write the molar mass of each compound in terms of the unknown atomic mass, equate mn, and solve the two equations.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using 22.4 L per mole for a solid or a liquid.

• Forgetting to multiply by the number of atoms per molecule.

• Mixing mg and g, cm and m, litres and mL.

• Writing the average atomic mass as a simple average of the isotope masses.

Completed notes – Type 5

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'The number of significant figures in ...' is asked for a given number such as 0.00340.

• A calculation is given and the answer must be quoted with the right number of significant figures.

• Statements about weight, mass, volume and density, or the laws of chemical combination, are given.

2. Formulae and laws

Which digits are significant? (three rules)

All non-zero digits; zeros between non-zero digits; trailing zeros after a decimal point. Leading zeros are not significant.

Significant figures in a product or quotient

Same as the quantity with the fewest significant figures

Significant figures in a sum or difference

Same number of decimal places as the quantity with the fewest decimal places

Number of significant figures in 0.00340; in 15.0; in 50000.020×10−3

3; 3; 8

Exact numbers (such as counting, 100%, conversion factors by definition)

Infinite significant figures; they do not limit the answer

Weight versus mass

Mass is the amount of matter (kg, constant); weight is the force of gravity on it (N, changes with place)

3. Concepts and skills

How do you quote a result of 0.02858×0.1120.5702?

Use the fewest significant figures among the data (0.112 has three), so the answer is 0.00561.

Why do trailing zeros in 15.0 count?

A zero after the decimal point shows the precision of the measurement.

How are standard-form numbers counted?

Count only the digits before the power of ten; the power of ten is not counted.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Counting leading zeros as significant.

• Using the decimal-place rule for a product (or the significant-figure rule for a sum).

• Rounding in the middle of a calculation instead of at the end.
