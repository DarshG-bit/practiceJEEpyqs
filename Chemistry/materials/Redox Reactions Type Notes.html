Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• A solution of KMnO4 or K2Cr2O7 is titrated against Fe2+, Mohr's salt, oxalic acid or H2O2 and a molarity, volume or strength is asked.

• The purity (percentage) of H2O2, MnO2 or an iron salt in a sample is asked from the volume of titrant.

• Iodine is liberated from Cu2+, Cl2 or iodate and titrated with 'hypo' (Na2S2O3) using starch.

2. Formulae and laws

End point of a redox titration

n1M1V1=n2M2V2 (equivalents of oxidant = equivalents of reductant)

Normality; strength in g/L

N=n-factor×M; strength =M×molar mass

MnO4− in acidic medium

MnO4−+8H++5e−→Mn2++4H2O; n-factor 5

Cr2O72− in acidic medium

Cr2O72−+14H++6e−→2Cr3++7H2O; n-factor 6

n-factors of the reductants

Fe2+→Fe3+: 1; C2O42−→2CO2: 2; FeC2O4: 3; H2O2→O2: 2

Basic medium: CrO42− with S2O32−

CrO42−→Cr(OH)4−: n=3; S2O32−→SO42−: n=8

Iodometry

2S2O32−+I2→S4O62−+2I−; 2Cu2++4I−→Cu2I2+I2

Iodide with iodate

5I−+IO3−+6H+→3I2+3H2O

Molar masses to remember

Mohr's salt FeSO4(NH4)2SO4⋅6H2O: 392; H2C2O4⋅2H2O: 126; KMnO4: 158; H2O2: 34

Percentage purity

mass of pure substancemass of sample×100

3. Concepts and skills

What is the quickest way to solve any redox titration?

Find the n-factor of the oxidant and of the reductant, then equate n×M×V on both sides.

Why is KMnO4 called a self-indicator?

The purple MnO4− turns colourless as Mn2+; the first permanent pink colour marks the end point.

How do you handle a mixture of FeC2O4, Fe2(C2O4)3, FeSO4 and Fe2(SO4)3?

Add the electrons lost by each reducing part (every Fe2+ gives 1, every oxalate gives 2); Fe3+ in the sulphate does not react.

How is a question with iodine and hypo solved?

Follow the chain: the oxidising agent liberates I2 from I−, and the moles of I2 equal half the moles of hypo used.

What does 'volume left in the burette' mean?

Burette volume minus the volume used at the end point.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the n-factor of KMnO4 as 5 in basic or neutral medium (it is 3 there).

• Using the molarity without multiplying by the n-factor.

• Forgetting that Cr2O72− has two chromium atoms (6 electrons, not 3).

• Using Fe2(SO4)3 as a reductant.

• Mixing the molar mass of oxalic acid with that of its dihydrate.

3. Concepts and skills

How do you find the basicity of an unknown acid?

Put the data in naMaVa=nbMbVb and solve for na.

Why does Na2CO3 show two end points?

It is neutralised in two steps, first to HCO3− (phenolphthalein), then to H2CO3 (methyl orange).

How do you choose the burette reading in a titration?

Use the concordant readings (the ones that agree within about 0.1 mL) and average them.

What happens with a monobasic acid of known density?

Find the moles required from the base, convert to mass with the molar mass, and divide by the density for the volume.

How is excess Ca(OH)2 handled?

Neutralise it with acid; the difference from the amount taken gives what reacted with CO2.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Forgetting the acidity of Ba(OH)2 or Ca(OH)2 (2).

• Taking H3PO3 as tribasic (it is dibasic) or H3PO2 as dibasic (monobasic).

• Dividing by the volume of only one solution after mixing.

• Using the full carbonate for the phenolphthalein end point.

• Using the sum of the two burette readings instead of the net acid consumed in Kjeldahl.

Completed notes – Type 3

1. How do I spot this type?

• Two statements (or an assertion and a reason) about indicators, primary standards or a titration are given and the correct option is asked.

• A graph of pH or conductance against the volume of NaOH added is to be chosen.

• 'Which will not be required', 'which cannot be used as a primary standard' or 'why heating is needed' is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

Phenolphthalein

Colourless in acid, pink in base; range pH 8.3–10; use for weak acid with strong base

Methyl orange

Red in acid, yellow in base; range pH 3.1–4.4; use for strong acid with weak base

Redox indicators

Sensitive to the change in oxidation potential, not pH (KMnO4 is a self-indicator; starch for iodine)

Primary standard

Pure, stable in air, not hygroscopic, high molar mass, fixed formula. Examples: oxalic acid dihydrate, KHP, Na2CO3, K2Cr2O7, borax

Not primary standards

NaOH, KOH (hygroscopic, absorb CO2), KMnO4, HCl, Na2S2O3; standardise them against a primary standard

KMnO4 titrations

Use dil. H2SO4, never HCl (Cl− is oxidised to Cl2) or HNO3 (an oxidant)

Oxalic acid with KMnO4

Warm to about 60∘C at the start; Mn2+ formed catalyses the reaction. No heating for Fe2+

Decomposition of KMnO4 at 513K

2KMnO4→K2MnO4+MnO2+O2

Strong acid with strong base

pH rises slowly, then jumps sharply near pH 7 at the equivalence point

Conductometric titration of a weak acid with NaOH

Conductance rises slowly up to the end point and sharply after it

3. Concepts and skills

Why can an indicator work for acid–base titrations but not for redox ones?

Acid–base indicators respond to pH; redox titrations are followed by a change in oxidation potential.

Why must the concentration of KOH be checked before use?

It absorbs moisture and CO2 from air, so its strength changes.

Why is potassium hydrogen phthalate a good standard for NaOH?

It is pure, stable, non-hygroscopic and has a high molar mass; phenolphthalein shows the end point.

How do you choose an indicator?

Its colour change must lie in the pH range of the steep part of the titration curve.

What is the colour change in KMnO4 titrations?

Colourless to a permanent pale pink at the end point.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using HCl to acidify a permanganate titration.

• Calling NaOH or KMnO4 a primary standard.

• Confusing the colours of phenolphthalein and methyl orange in acid and base.

• Taking Na2Cr2O7 for the primary standard (the usual one is K2Cr2O7).

• Reading a weak-acid curve as if it began at pH 1.

3. Concepts and skills

Which method is fastest for finding a coefficient?

Equate the electrons lost and gained (the change in oxidation number method), then balance the rest by charge and atoms.

How do you balance in basic medium?

Balance as in acid, then add as many OH− as H+ on both sides to form water.

Do water molecules of crystallisation count?

Yes. For KMnO4 with ferrous ammonium sulphate hexahydrate, the water produced is that from the reaction plus the water of crystallisation of the reacting salt.

What is the change in oxidation state for MnO4− going to Mn2+?

From +7 to +2, a change of 5.

How do you check a balanced equation?

Count each atom and the net charge on both sides.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Balancing atoms but not charge.

• Using H+ in a basic medium equation.

• Forgetting the two Cr atoms in dichromate.

• Counting only the water formed and ignoring the water of crystallisation.

• Equalising electrons by adding instead of multiplying the half-reactions.

A reaction is redox if

the oxidation number of at least one element changes

Types of redox reactions

Combination A+B→AB; decomposition AB→A+B; displacement A+BC→AC+B; disproportionation

Only oxidising agents (highest oxidation state)

KMnO4, K2Cr2O7, HNO3, conc. H2SO4, HClO4, S2O82−, F2

Only reducing agents (lowest oxidation state)

H2S, I−, Zn, Na, H2

Both oxidising and reducing (intermediate state)

H2O2, SO2, NO2, HNO2, Fe2+, Sn2+

Strength from E∘ (reduction potentials)

Higher E∘: stronger oxidising agent, weaker reducing agent of its reduced form

Spontaneous reaction

Ecell∘=Ereduction∘−Eoxidation∘>0

3. Concepts and skills

How do you decide if a species can act as an oxidising agent?

It must be able to lower its oxidation number. The element at its lowest state cannot.

Which species can act both ways?

Those whose element is in an intermediate oxidation state, such as H2O2 (O at −1).

How do you compare oxidising power from a list of half-reactions?

Arrange by E∘ for reduction: the larger the value, the stronger the oxidising agent.

How is the type of the reaction in Zn+2[Au(CN)2]−→… named?

Zinc displaces gold from its complex, and the oxidation numbers change, so it is a redox displacement reaction.

When is H2O2 a reducing agent?

With stronger oxidants such as HOCl, KMnO4 or I2 in base, it is oxidised to O2.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Calling every reaction with an ion a redox reaction without checking oxidation numbers.

• Choosing the species at its highest state as a reducing agent.

• Taking the smaller E∘ as the stronger oxidising agent.

• Forgetting that F2 has no positive oxidation state.

• Using oxidation potentials as if they were reduction potentials.

Definition

One element in a single species is both oxidised and reduced (the same species gives a higher and a lower oxidation state)

Condition

The element must be in an intermediate oxidation state

Halogens in alkali

Cl2+2OH−→ClO−+Cl−+H2O (cold); 3Cl2+6OH−→ClO3−+5Cl−+3H2O (hot). F2 does not

Manganate in acid

3MnO42−+4H+→2MnO4−+MnO2+2H2O (+6→+7 and +4)

Copper(I)

2Cu+→Cu2++Cu

Sulphur and phosphorus in alkali

2H2O2→2H2O+O2; 2NO2+2OH−→NO2−+NO3−+H2O

F2, K+, Ag, Cl−, ClO4− (lowest or highest state)

Chlorate

4ClO3−→3ClO4−+Cl− on heating

3. Concepts and skills

How do you test a reaction for disproportionation?

Write the oxidation number of the element on the reactant side and check that it appears at a higher and a lower value in the products.

Why does ClO4− not disproportionate?

Chlorine is at its highest state (+7) and cannot be oxidised further.

Why can halogens other than fluorine disproportionate?

They exist in the states −1, 0 and positive states; fluorine shows only −1 and 0.

When is a reaction with two reactants not a disproportionation?

When the higher and the lower oxidation states come from two different species (an ordinary redox or comproportionation reaction), the same species is not both oxidised and reduced.

What is the difference between the two ions formed by Mn(VI)?

+7 (permanganate) and +4 (MnO2): a difference of 3.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Calling a reaction a disproportionation when two different elements are oxidised and reduced.

• Counting Ag or K+ as disproportionating.

• Assuming F2 behaves like Cl2 in alkali.

• Missing that KMnO4 heated gives manganate and MnO2 from different atoms of the same species.

Rule

Sum of the oxidation numbers = net charge of the species

Fixed values

F −1; alkali metals +1; alkaline earth +2; H +1 (in hydrides −1); O −2

Oxygen exceptions

Peroxides −1; superoxides −1/2; OF2 +2

Potassium oxides

K2O, K2O2, KO2: potassium is +1 in all (oxygen is −2, −1, −1/2)

Nitrogen

NO +2; NO2 +4; N2O +1; NO3− +5

Metals in oxo-anions

K2Cr2O7 Cr +6; KMnO4 Mn +7; K2FeO4 Fe +6

Phosphorus acids

H4P2O7 +5; H4P2O5 +3; H4P2O6 +4

Polythionic acid H2SxO6

Average =10x; real states: terminal S +5, bridging S 0

Valence electrons from the lowest oxidation number

Lowest oxidation number −n means 8−n valence electrons; −2 gives 6

3. Concepts and skills

How do you find the oxidation number of an element in a complex ion?

Use the net charge and the known oxidation numbers of the other atoms or ligands.

Why are oxidation numbers of S in polythionic acids written as 'state(s)'?

The sulphur atoms are not equivalent: the terminal ones are +5 and the middle ones are 0.

What happens to chromium when dichromate is treated with base?

It becomes chromate; the oxidation state stays +6 because the change is not redox.

What does thiosulphate become with a strong oxidant such as permanganate in mildly alkaline medium?

Sulphate, with sulphur at +6.

How can the group of an element be inferred from the lowest oxidation number?

A lowest state of −2 means group 16 with six valence electrons.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Taking oxygen as −2 in peroxides and superoxides.

• Using the average oxidation state when the question asks for the state of each atom.

• Forgetting the sign and the charge on an ion.

• Treating dichromate to chromate as redox.
