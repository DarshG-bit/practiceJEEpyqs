Completed notes – Type 1

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Fastest SN2', 'decreasing order towards SN1' or 'will not show SN1' is written in the stem.

• A halide is treated with AgNO3 and the one that gives a precipitate (most readily) is asked.

• Two statements or an assertion and a reason are given on solvent, nucleophile, racemisation or inversion.

2. Formulae and laws

SN2

rate =k[RX][Nu], one step, inversion; CH3X>1∘>2∘≫3∘

SN1

rate =k[RX], two steps, carbocation, racemisation; 3°>2°>1°; allyl, benzyl high

Leaving group

I−>Br−>Cl−>F− (weaker base = better leaving group)

Solvent

polar protic favours SN1; polar aprotic (acetone, DMF) favours SN2

Not reactive

vinyl, aryl and bridgehead halides give no SN1 or SN2

Precipitate test

RX + AgNO3 → AgX: fast when a stable carbocation forms

3. Concepts and skills

Why do 3° halides fail in SN2?

Three alkyl groups block back-side attack on the carbon, so the transition state is too crowded.

Why is benzyl chloride reactive in both SN1 and SN2?

The benzyl cation is stabilised by resonance, and the sp2-like transition state is stabilised by the ring.

How does an EWG (NO2) or EDG (OMe) on the ring change the SN1 rate of a benzyl chloride?

EDG at para stabilises the cation (faster); EWG destabilises it (slower).

Why does a lone pair on N next to the C–Cl bond speed up hydrolysis?

The lone pair attacks intramolecularly (neighbouring-group participation) to form a cyclic ion, which water then opens.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using the SN2 order for an SN1 question.

• Treating a vinyl or aryl chloride as an alkyl halide.

• Saying SN1 always gives complete racemisation (ion pairs give some inversion).

• Choosing a polar protic solvent for SN2 speed.

Completed notes – Type 2

1. How do I spot this type?

• Cl2/hν or Br2/hν is used and 'major product' or 'number of monochloro derivatives' is asked.

• An alkene is treated with HX, HOX or X2, or an alcohol with SOCl2, PCl5 or HX.

• A multi-step sequence starts from toluene or an alkane and ends at a halo-acid or halo-compound.

2. Formulae and laws

Radical halogenation

R−H+X2→hνR−X+HX; Br2: 3°>2°>1° (selective), Cl2 less selective

Counting isomers

number of non-equivalent H sets, then add enantiomers and cis/trans of each product

Markovnikov

CH3CH=CH2+HBr→ CH3CHBrCH3; with peroxide: CH3CH2CH2Br

From alcohols

ROH+SOCl2→ RCl+SO2+HCl; HX/ZnCl2 (Lucas): 3°>2°>1°

Toluene

Cl2/hν → benzyl chloride; Cl2/Fe → o/p chlorotoluene

Boiling point

RI > RBr > RCl > RF; straight chain > branched

3. Concepts and skills

Why is bromination more selective than chlorination?

The H-abstraction by Br· is endothermic and has a late transition state, so it follows the stability of the radical.

How many monochloro derivatives does 2,2,5,5-tetramethylhexane give?

Find the sets of equivalent H: terminal CH3 groups and the ring of CH2 groups; then check for chirality.

Why does a peroxide reverse the addition of HBr only?

The radical chain adds Br· first to give the more stable radical; HCl and HI do not sustain the chain.

Why is side-chain halogenation done with light, ring halogenation with Fe?

Light gives radicals that attack the benzylic C–H; FeCl3 makes an electrophile that attacks the ring.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Forgetting stereoisomers when counting monochloro products.

• Applying Markovnikov addition when a peroxide is present.

• Halogenating the ring when light and heat are given (it is the side chain).

• Using Cl2 selectivity where Br2 is given.

Completed notes – Type 3

1. How do I spot this type?

• KCN, AgCN, KNO2 or AgNO2 appears with an alkyl halide, or 'isocyanide' is mentioned.

• An alkyl halide reacts with an alkoxide, ammonia or an anion and the structure of the product is asked.

• A dihalide or halo-compound is converted in two or three steps, and a count (π electrons, atoms) is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

KCN

RX+KCN→ R−CN (nitrile, ionic salt attacks by C)

AgCN

RX+AgCN→ R−NC (isocyanide, covalent salt attacks by N)

KNO2

RX+KNO2→ R−ONO (alkyl nitrite)

AgNO2

RX+AgNO2→ R−NO2 (nitroalkane)

Williamson

RX+R'ONa→ R−O−R' (1° RX only; 3° RX eliminates)

Nitrile hydrolysis

R−CN→H3O+RCOOH (one more carbon than RX)

3. Concepts and skills

Why does KCN give R–CN but AgCN gives R–NC?

KCN is ionic, so the carbon (more nucleophilic site) attacks. AgCN is covalent, so only the N lone pair is free to attack.

Why are 3° halides unsuitable for Williamson ether synthesis?

The alkoxide is a strong base and removes a β-H, so the alkene forms by E2.

What does excess NH3 do with an alkyl halide?

It suppresses further alkylation, so the primary amine is the main product.

How is a ring formed from a halo-compound with a nucleophilic site?

Intramolecular SN2 closes 5- and 6-membered rings fastest.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Swapping the products of KCN and AgCN.

• Writing nitroalkane for KNO2 (it is the nitrite ester) and nitrite ester for AgNO2.

• Forgetting the extra carbon of the nitrile in counting questions.

• Using a 3° halide in an ether synthesis.

Completed notes – Type 4

1. How do I spot this type?

• Alcoholic KOH, NaOEt or t-BuOK is given with a halide and 'major product' is asked.

• Aqueous and alcoholic KOH are contrasted, or ks/ke (the ratio of substitution to elimination) is mentioned.

• 'Maximum number of alkenes' or 'order of reactivity towards dehydrohalogenation' is asked.

2. Formulae and laws

E2

one step, rate =k[RX][B], anti-periplanar H and X

E1

two steps via carbocation, rate =k[RX]; 3°>2°>1°

Zaitsev

the more substituted alkene is the major product (small bases)

Hofmann

the less substituted alkene with bulky bases (t-BuOK) or when F is the leaving group

Conditions

aq. KOH → ROH; alc. KOH, Δ → alkene

Ratio

μ=ks/ke is larger for a small nucleophile (EtO⁻) than for a bulky one (t-BuO⁻)

3. Concepts and skills

Why does alcoholic KOH favour elimination?

In alcohol, RO⁻ is a stronger base and is less solvated than OH⁻ in water, so it removes the β-H.

Why does t-BuOK give the less substituted alkene?

The bulky base reaches the more accessible, less hindered β-H.

How do you count alkenes from a halide?

List every β-carbon with H, draw each alkene and add cis/trans isomers.

Why does a 3° halide with a weak base still eliminate?

The carbocation (E1) loses a β-H easily, and substitution is blocked by steric crowding.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Giving the substitution product with alcoholic KOH.

• Applying Zaitsev when a bulky base is used.

• Missing cis/trans isomers when counting alkenes.

• Forgetting that fluoride as the leaving group gives the Hofmann product.

Completed notes – Type 5

1. How do I spot this type?

• Mg in dry ether or THF is the first reagent, or D2O / H2O follows an organometallic step.

• Na in dry ether is used with one or two halides.

• A List I – List II match of reactions with their names is given.

2. Formulae and laws

Grignard

RX+Mg→dryetherRMgX; RMgX+H2O→ RH (D2O gives RD)

With carbonyls

HCHO → 1° alcohol; RCHO → 2° alcohol; ketone or ester → 3° alcohol; CO2 → RCOOH

Wurtz

2RX+2Na→ R−R+2NaX

Fittig

2ArX+2Na→ Ar−Ar+2NaX; Wurtz–Fittig: ArX+RX+2Na→ Ar−R

Finkelstein

RCl+NaI→acetoneRI+NaCl

Sandmeyer

ArN2+X−→CuXArX+N2; Gattermann: Cu powder + HX

3. Concepts and skills

Why must a Grignard reagent be made in dry ether?

RMgX is a strong base and is destroyed by water or any acidic H, giving the alkane.

Why do compounds with –OH, –COOH or –NH₂ not form a Grignard reagent?

The acidic H protonates the carbanion as soon as it forms.

How many halides RBr give 2-methylbutane through Mg and H2O?

Each RBr whose carbon skeleton equals 2-methylbutane: place Br on every non-equivalent carbon.

Why does a dihalide with excess Mg give a di-Grignard?

Each C–X bond reacts separately, so two equivalents of Mg are used.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Using aqueous conditions for a Grignard reaction.

• Mixing up Wurtz (alkyl) and Fittig (aryl) coupling.

• Forgetting that D₂O puts D on the carbon that carried Mg.

• Writing the Sandmeyer product as ArH.

Completed notes – Type 6

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Order of reactivity in electrophilic substitution' of chlorobenzene, toluene, nitrobenzene and benzene is asked.

• AlCl3 with an alkyl or acyl halide is used on a substituted benzene ring.

• A halo-arene bearing a side chain with a halide is heated with AlCl3 (intramolecular ring closure).

2. Formulae and laws

Directing effect

–X: deactivating but o/p directing (−I>+M)

Reactivity

OMe, CH3> benzene >Cl>NO2, COR

Friedel–Crafts

ArH+RCl→AlCl3ArR; ArH+RCOCl→AlCl3ArCOR

Fails when

the ring has NO2, CONH2, NH2 or other strongly deactivating groups

Product

para is major; ortho is hindered

Intramolecular

RCl on a side chain + AlCl3 closes a 5- or 6-membered ring

3. Concepts and skills

Why does Cl deactivate the ring yet direct o/p?

Its inductive pull makes the ring electron-poor overall, but its lone pair stabilises the o/p intermediate by resonance.

Why does Friedel–Crafts fail on aniline and nitrobenzene?

The NH₂ group binds AlCl3 and NO2 deactivates the ring too strongly.

How do you decide which ring position closes in an intramolecular reaction?

Count the atoms: 5- and 6-membered rings form, and the cation formed from the more substituted C–Cl is attacked.

Why is p- favoured over o- in the acylation of chlorobenzene?

The bulky acylium ion avoids the crowded position next to Cl.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Treating Cl as an activating group.

• Placing the acyl group meta in chlorobenzene.

• Forgetting carbocation rearrangement in Friedel–Crafts alkylation.

• Using Friedel–Crafts on a strongly deactivated ring.

Completed notes – Type 7

1. How do I spot this type?

• 'Reactivity towards nucleophilic substitution with aq. NaOH' of nitro-chlorobenzenes is asked.

• A halobenzene is converted to a phenol or a diazonium salt is converted to ArX.

• A reaction of phenol with Br2 is asked, or a mass is asked from a sequence of reactions.

2. Formulae and laws

SNAr

o/p NO2 groups make ArCl react with OH−; rate rises with the number of o/p NO2

Dow process

ArCl+NaOH(623K,300atm)→ArONa→H+ArOH

Sandmeyer

ArN2+→CuXArX(X=Cl,Br,CN); F, I and At are not made this way

Phenol + Br2

water → 2,4,6-tribromophenol; CS2, low T → p-bromophenol

Benzyne

ArCl+NaNH2→ ArNH2 (elimination–addition)

Moles

n=m/M and the ratio of the equation, then convert back to mass

3. Concepts and skills

Why is chlorobenzene inert to SN under ordinary conditions?

The C–Cl bond has partial double-bond character (resonance) and the carbon is sp2, so back-side attack is impossible.

Why does a meta NO2 not help?

It cannot delocalise the negative charge of the Meisenheimer intermediate onto its oxygen.

Why are halobenzenes made through the diazonium route and not directly?

The ring is deactivated and the position is controlled by the amine, so Sandmeyer places the halogen exactly.

How is the mass of a final product found in a sequence?

Convert the given mass to moles, follow the 1:1 steps, then multiply by the molar mass of the final product.

4. Mistakes to avoid

• Assuming meta nitro groups activate the ring.

• Writing Sandmeyer products for iodine and fluorine.

• Using the molar mass of the wrong compound in a mass calculation.

• Forgetting that phenol with Br2 water gives the tribromo product.
